Replacing high-maintenance plantings with species that fit the valley's future

A field guide for homeowners, arborists, landscapers and land managers. It matches each replacement to the soil, so the new planting survives both halves of the valley's year.

Southern Willamette Valley soils are saturated from November to April and dry from June to October. Most drought lists rate only the summer. Here, winter kills as many plants as drought does. Every species in this framework is rated on both.

How to use it

Start with the plant you want to replace and the soil it grows in. Choose a replacement that fits that soil, water it for three summers, then stop. Keep a simple record of what you plant and how it does each year. The ratings here are a starting point; real results should correct them. See the full process.

Three rules that shape every recommendation

  1. Start with the soil. A plant that is perfect for a dry slope dies in valley clay by February.
  2. Replace what needs replacing. A healthy tree that causes no problems is usually worth keeping.
  3. Water the first three summers, then stop. Survival is decided during establishment, not at planting.

Ash and emerald ash borer

Status, checked October 2026. Emerald ash borer (EAB) is confirmed in Clackamas, Marion, Multnomah, Washington and Yamhill counties, as close as Salem and Silverton. It is not confirmed in Lane County as of August 2026 reports. Ash here can still be protected before the beetle arrives, when treatment works best. Check the ODA page for the current status.

Field guidance

EAB signs to check
  • D-shaped exit holes, about 1/8 in wide.
  • Woodpecker damage: patches of light, flaked bark.
  • Vertical bark splits with S-shaped tunnels underneath.
  • Sprouts on the trunk or at the base.
  • Crown thinning from the top down.

A suspected find in Lane County would be a new county record. Report it to the Oregon Invasive Species Hotline before moving any wood.

Phased replacement: plant first, remove later
  1. Plant the successor now, in light: outside the ash dripline or in a canopy gap.
  2. Water it on the establishment schedule.
  3. Check the ash at each visit. Record dieback and signs.
  4. Remove the ash at 30% dieback, when EAB reaches Lane County, or when the successor gives useful shade (often year 5–8).
  5. Remove it on your schedule. Waiting until the tree dies makes removal costlier and more dangerous.
Quarantine and wood handling
  1. Ash, olive and white fringetree material cannot leave the five quarantined counties. This includes logs, chips, mulch, stumps, roots and branches. Hardwood firewood cannot leave either.
  2. Never bring ash material or hardwood firewood from those counties into Lane County.
  3. If Lane County is added, ash wood and chips must stay in the county. Plan for local disposal.
Removing dead or dying ash
  • EAB-killed ash turns brittle within one to two years of death. Limbs fail without warning.
  • Do not climb an ash with more than 50% dieback. Use a lift or crane.
Riparian and wetland sites

Oregon ash often grows in wetlands and along streams. Replace it with natives only on these sites: Pacific crabapple, native willows, Oregon white oak on drier edges, with red-osier dogwood and Pacific ninebark below. Swamp white oak is not native to Oregon. Work in wetlands and stream buffers may need permits.

When EAB is confirmed in Lane County
  1. Untreated ash can no longer wait. Decide on treatment or removal this season.
  2. Treat valuable, healthy trees this season if they are not yet treated.
  3. Re-check every recorded tree for dieback and signs.
  4. Check for quarantine changes before moving any ash wood.

First summer1075
Second summer211410
Third summer302821
  • Clay holds water, so intervals stretch; the risk there is root rot. Upland soils dry out fast, so intervals shorten.
  • Trees get 10 gallons per inch of trunk caliper. Shrubs get about 1 gallon per container gallon.
  • Drought-adapted species (manzanita, ceanothus, rockrose, rosemary and others) water 1.5 times less often for two summers, then get no summer water at all. Summer water after year two kills them more often than drought.
  • Heat wave, three or more days above 100 °F: water every planting once, in any year.

Hedgerows

A hedgerow is a mixed, layered planting in place of a single-species hedge. It screens, feeds wildlife and spreads pest risk across six to ten species. Use it where the site is at least 8 ft wide. Based on OSU Extension EM 8721, adapted to residential lots.

Plant count

Template A: wet clay, three rows, about 20 ft wide

RowPick 2–4 per rowNotes
Tall centerMalus fusca; Quercus garryana or Q. bicolor (one per 25–30 ft); Salix hookeriana on rural sitesDo not fill the center with oak. Use crabapple between oaks.
ScreenMorella californica; Physocarpus capitatus; Sambucus cerulea; Cornus sericea; Oemleria cerasiformisWax myrtle in every second or third slot. It is the only evergreen here.
EdgeSpiraea douglasii; Rosa nutkana; Symphoricarpos albus; Lonicera involucrataAll spread. Give them a defined strip and a mowed edge.

Family limit. Crabapple, ninebark, osoberry, rose and spiraea are all in the rose family. Keep them to 3 plants in 10 or fewer. A test mix built mostly from these natives reached about 70% one family.

Template B: narrow residential, two staggered rows, 8–10 ft wide

RowSpeciesNotes
BackAlternate Morella californica with Physocarpus capitatus (Groups 1–2) or Osmanthus × burkwoodii (Groups 2–3)Direct replacement for a laurel or arborvitae hedge.
FrontRibes sanguineum; Cornus sericea 'Isanti'; Berberis aquifolium; Rosa nutkana away from pathsInformal. Not for a formal, sheared look.

Care

  • Mulch 3–4 in deep. Keep a mowed strip around the planting for weeds, rodents and fire.
  • Keep hedgerows out of the first 5 ft around buildings. No resinous evergreens next to walls.
  • Watering ends after year three. The hedgerow closes in year four to eight, so keep edge and weed control going until then.

Framework

Two axes, not one

Each species is placed by its summer drought tolerance and its winter-wet clay tolerance. The plants in the top right handle both seasons. The bottom right is the trap: Mediterranean shrubs that survive August and drown in February.

nameTier A, provennameTier B, trialnameTier C or rejected

Soil groups

Group 1, valley bottom clay. Bethel, Dayton, Natroy, Amity and Concord series. Poorly drained, with a perched water table from November to April. Winter-wet tolerance decides. Use a 12–18 in berm for anything rated "Caution."

Group 2, alluvial silt loam. Malabon, Coburg, Salem, Chehalis and Newberg series. Deep and well drained. Nearly the whole palette fits.

Group 3, footslope red clay. Jory, Nekia, Bellpine and Dixonville series. Sheds water, often shallow to bedrock, dry in summer. The right home for California and Mediterranean species. Check rooting depth before large trees.

Should it be replaced?

Tiers

A Proven
Documented performance in the valley. Use freely in the right soil group.
B Trial
Sound logic but unproven here, or carrying a named risk. Keep to 10% or less of any planting.
C Caution
Fails the low-input standard. Use only with a clear reason and a backup plan.
Reject
Do not plant. Kept on the list with the reason, so the decision is not argued again.
Promotion
A trial species earns Tier A after several plantings reach year three with 80% or more survival. A species below 70% survival drops a tier. Mower damage and missed watering do not count against the species.

Diversity: the 10-20-30 rule

No species above 10% of all plants installed, no genus above 20%, no family above 30%. Two limits bind first here. Oaks lead the wet-clay list, so the oak genus fills fast. Most wet-site natives are in the rose family, so that family fills fast in wet hedgerows. Replacing an ash monoculture with an oak or rose-family monoculture solves nothing.
