A holistic approach to tree removal and replacement in the southern Willamette Valley

Removal is a single step in the urban canopy succession. This guide treats removal decisions, replanting choices, and follow up care as parts of a single process. It highlights the importance of maintaining urban canopy and favoring resilient, low maintenance planting,

Replanting starts in the soil. Southern Willamette Valley soils are saturated from November to April and dry from June to October. Winter saturation causes as many plant losses as summer drought. Every species in this guide is rated for both.

Choose a soil group to see the species that fit it, or look up the soil at a site.

Elements of the approach

Each removal and replacement is assessed across six elements. Treating them together avoids the common failures: removing a tree that could have been kept, leaving a gap for years, or planting a species the soil will kill.

The tree or planting
Condition, structural risk, pests and disease, and value. Retention, pruning or treatment are considered before removal.
The site
Soil group, drainage, sun, space, overhead and underground utilities, and water access.
Neighboring trees
Root zones and canopy of the trees that stay. Removal work must not damage them.
Removed material
Wood, chips and stumps. Reuse chips as mulch where possible, and follow quarantine rules for ash.
The replacement
Species matched to the soil, the space and the future climate, with diversity across the site.
Time
Canopy continuity, three summers of establishment, and annual monitoring after that.

Core principles

  1. Remove only where warranted. A healthy tree that causes no problems is usually worth keeping. Consider pruning or treatment first.
  2. Plan the replacement before the removal. Choose the species and the location first. Where possible, plant the successor before the old tree comes out.
  3. Match species to soil. A species suited to a dry slope can fail in saturated valley clay within one winter.
  4. Protect what stays. Keep equipment and debris off the root zones of the remaining trees.
  5. Irrigate for three summers, then stop. Survival is determined during establishment, not at planting.
  6. Monitor and record. Annual checks show what survives on each soil and turn these ratings into local evidence. See the full process.

The soil survey is a starting point. It is mapped for areas of several acres, so it can miss the conditions on a single lot. Urban lots are often cut, filled or graded. Confirm on site with the drain test: dig 18 in and fill the hole with water. If it has not drained in an hour, use Group 1.

Field procedures

Signs of EAB infestation
  • D-shaped exit holes, about 1/8 in wide.
  • Woodpecker damage: patches of light, flaked bark.
  • Vertical bark splits with S-shaped tunnels underneath.
  • Sprouts on the trunk or at the base.
  • Crown thinning from the top down.

A suspected find in Lane County would be a new county record. Report it to the Oregon Invasive Species Hotline before moving any wood.

Phased replacement procedure
  1. Plant the successor now, in light: outside the ash dripline or in a canopy gap.
  2. Water it on the establishment schedule.
  3. Check the ash at each visit. Record dieback and signs.
  4. Remove the ash at 30% dieback, when EAB reaches Lane County, or when the successor gives useful shade (often year 5–8).
  5. Remove it on your schedule. Waiting until the tree dies makes removal costlier and more dangerous.
Quarantine and wood handling
  1. Ash, olive and white fringetree material cannot leave the five quarantined counties without an ODA compliance agreement. This includes logs, chips, mulch, stumps, roots and branches. Hardwood firewood cannot leave either.
  2. Never bring ash material or hardwood firewood from those counties into Lane County.
  3. If Lane County is added, ash wood and chips must stay in the county. Plan for local disposal.
Removal of dead and declining ash
  • EAB-killed ash turns brittle within one to two years of death. Limbs fail without warning.
  • Do not climb an ash with more than 50% dieback. Use a lift or crane.
Riparian and wetland sites

Oregon ash often grows in wetlands and along streams. Replace it with natives only on these sites: Pacific crabapple, native willows, Oregon white oak on drier edges, with red-osier dogwood and Pacific ninebark below. Swamp white oak is not native to Oregon. Work in wetlands and stream buffers may need permits.

Response to a confirmed Lane County detection
  1. Untreated ash can no longer wait. Decide on treatment or removal this season.
  2. Treat valuable, healthy trees this season if they are not yet treated.
  3. Re-check every recorded tree for dieback and signs.
  4. Check for quarantine changes before moving any ash wood.

  • Clay holds water, so intervals stretch; the risk there is root rot. Upland soils dry out fast, so intervals shorten.
  • Trees get 10 gallons per inch of trunk caliper. Shrubs get about 1 gallon per container gallon.
  • Drought-adapted species (manzanita, ceanothus, rockrose, rosemary and others) water 1.5 times less often for two summers, then get no summer water at all. Summer water after year two kills them more often than drought.
  • Heat wave, three or more days above 100 °F: water every planting once, in any year.

Hedgerow design

When a single-species hedge is removed, a hedgerow is a strong replacement: a mixed, layered planting in its place. It screens, feeds wildlife and spreads pest risk across six to ten species. Use it where the site is at least 8 ft wide. Based on OSU Extension EM 8721, adapted to residential lots.

Family limit. Crabapple, ninebark, osoberry, rose and spiraea are all in the rose family. Keep them to 3 plants in 10 or fewer. A test mix built mostly from these natives reached about 70% one family.

Establishment and maintenance

  • Mulch 3–4 in deep. Keep a mowed strip around the planting for weeds, rodents and fire.
  • Keep hedgerows out of the first 5 ft around buildings. No resinous evergreens next to walls.
  • Watering ends after year three. The hedgerow closes in year four to eight, so keep edge and weed control going until then.

Decision framework

Summer drought and winter saturation tolerance

Each species is placed by its summer drought tolerance and its tolerance of saturated winter clay. Species in the upper right tolerate both conditions. Species in the lower right, mainly Mediterranean shrubs, tolerate summer drought but fail in saturated winter soils.

Soil groups of the southern Willamette Valley

Group 1, valley bottom clay. Bashaw, Dayton, Natroy, Amity, Concord and Waldo series. Poorly or somewhat poorly drained, with a perched water table from November to April. Winter-wet tolerance decides. Use a 12–18 in berm for anything rated "Caution."

Group 2, alluvial silt loam. Malabon, Coburg, Salem, Chehalis, Newberg and Cloquato series. Well to moderately well drained alluvium on terraces and floodplains, mostly silt loam to silty clay loam. Nearly the whole palette fits.

Group 3, footslope red clay. Jory, Nekia, Bellpine and Dixonville series. Sheds water. Nekia, Bellpine and Dixonville are moderately deep (20–40 in) to bedrock; Jory is very deep. Dry in summer. The right home for California and Mediterranean species. Check rooting depth before large trees.

Removal and replacement classes

Species tiers

A Proven
Documented performance in the valley. Use freely in the right soil group.
B Trial
Sound logic but unproven here, or carrying a named risk. Keep to 10% or less of any planting.
C Caution
Fails the low-input standard. Use only with a clear reason and a backup plan.
Reject
Do not plant. Kept on the list with the reason, so the decision is not argued again.
Promotion
A trial species earns Tier A after several plantings reach year three with 80% or more survival. A species below 70% survival drops a tier. Mower damage and missed watering do not count against the species.

Species diversity limits (10-20-30 rule)

No species above 10% of all plants installed, no genus above 20%, no family above 30%. Two limits bind first here. Oaks lead the wet-clay list, so the oak genus fills fast. Most wet-site natives are in the rose family, so that family fills fast in wet hedgerows. Replacing an ash monoculture with an oak or rose-family monoculture solves nothing.
